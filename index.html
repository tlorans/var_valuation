Step 00 · The benchmark

The DCF you already know

Damodaran's DCF is the workhorse of every valuation course and every banker's model:

$$V_t = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{E_t[D_{t+j}]}{(1+r)^j}$$
(0)

Terms · WACC, Gordon growth, and the notation of this course

WACC — weighted average cost of capital: the blended required return of a firm's investors (cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt, weighted by their shares of the capital structure). In a DCF it plays "the" discount rate applied to every future cash flow. Gordon growth model — the formula $D_{t+1}/(r - g)$: if cash flows start at $D_{t+1}$ and grow at a constant rate $g$ forever, discounted at a constant rate $r > g$, the infinite sum collapses to that single ratio. It is where DCF spreadsheets get their terminal value, and — remember this formula — Step 06 shows it is a special case of the general model. Notation: $D_{t+j}$ is the cash flow (dividend) $j$ periods ahead; $E_t[\cdot]$ means "average over the possible outcomes, using only information available at time $t$" (the $t$ subscript is the timing of knowledge); $\sum$ is summation over $j$.

with a constant WACC $r$, and then a Gordon terminal value $D_{t+1}/(r-g)$ bolted on at the end. Two assumptions are buried inside that innocuous-looking line:

1

One $r$ for all horizons

A flat, constant discount curve. The rate you use to discount year 1 is the rate you use to discount year 40.

2

Expectation of the ratio ≈ ratio of expectations

Cash flows and discount rates are treated as independent. The discount factor simply slides out of the expectation.

Both are wrong, and Ang–Liu shows how wrong.

Step 01 · The definition

The correct definition of value

Value is the expectation of the discounted cash flow — with the discount factor inside the expectation:

$$V_t = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} E_t\left[ D_{t+j} \cdot \exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{t+i}\right) \right]$$
(1)

where $r_{t+i}$ is the (log) one-period expected return from $t+i-1$ to $t+i$ — itself random as of time $t$.

The whole paper in one line

Damodaran's formula pulls the discount factor out of the expectation. That is only legitimate if $r$ is deterministic. Once expected returns move, $E[XY] \neq E[X]\,E[Y]$.

Everything that follows is just bookkeeping to make equation (1) computable — without ever pulling the discount factor back out.

Step 02 · Growth form

Write cash flows in growth form

Let $g_{t+i} = \log(D_{t+i}/D_{t+i-1})$. Then $D_{t+j} = D_t \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^j g_{t+i}\right)$, and (1) becomes

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} E_t\left[ \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j} \left(g_{t+i} - r_{t+i}\right)\right) \right]$$
(2)

Value is now a price–dividend ratio: a sum over horizons of the expected exponential of cumulated growth-minus-discount-rate. One strip per horizon — exactly the objects that trade, in fragmented form, in the dividend-derivatives market.

Step 03 · Dynamics

The VAR: what it is, and why it is the right tool

Why one model for both cash flows and expected returns?

Value is the expectation of a product — cash flow times discount factor. The expectation of a product depends on the joint distribution, not on each piece separately. If you forecast cash flows in one model and expected returns in another, you have no covariance term, no consistent horizon dynamics, and the two forecasts can even contradict each other. Step 04 will make the covariance term precise: $-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\sum g, \sum r)$ sits inside the price itself. A single model that generates both series jointly is not elegance — it is the minimum required to price the product correctly.

So we need one statistical engine that describes how cash flow growth $g_t$ and expected returns $r_t$ move, forecast each other, revert, and co-move. The paper's choice is a vector autoregression.

What, concretely, is a VAR?

A VAR is just a system of ordinary linear regressions run simultaneously. Take the variables you care about, stack them into a vector, and regress each one on all of them, lagged one period. With two variables, $x$ and $y$, a VAR(1) is literally two regressions:

$$x_{t+1} = a_1 + b_{11} x_t + b_{12} y_t + u_{t+1} \qquad\qquad y_{t+1} = a_2 + b_{21} x_t + b_{22} y_t + v_{t+1}$$
(3a)

"Symmetric" treatment: nothing is labeled a cause or an effect. $y$ can forecast $x$ ($b_{12} \neq 0$) and $x$ can forecast $y$ ($b_{21} \neq 0$). Writing the pair as a vector gives the compact form the paper uses — with the state vector stacked and the coefficients gathered into matrices:

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} x_{t+1} \\ y_{t+1} \end{pmatrix}}_{X_{t+1}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mu} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} b_{11} & b_{12} \\ b_{21} & b_{22} \end{pmatrix}}_{\Phi} \begin{pmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} u_{t+1} \\ v_{t+1} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{shocks}}$$
(3b)

Three named objects, each with a job. $\mu$ pulls everything toward long-run averages. $\Phi$ is the persistence matrix: its diagonal says how much each variable remembers itself; its off-diagonals say which variables forecast which. The shocks arrive every period, with a covariance matrix $\Sigma\Sigma'$ whose off-diagonal entries say which variables get hit together. Gaussian shocks + linear dynamics is what makes every future horizon computable in closed form.

Forecasting with a VAR is recursive bookkeeping

One step ahead: $E_t[X_{t+1}] = \mu + \Phi X_t$. Two steps: plug the one-step forecast back in, $E_t[X_{t+2}] = \mu + \Phi E_t[X_{t+1}] = (I + \Phi)\mu + \Phi^2 X_t$. By $j$ steps, $E_t[X_{t+j}] = (I + \Phi + \dots + \Phi^{j-1})\mu + \Phi^j X_t$. Two consequences: today's state dies out at the speed of $\Phi^j$ (the eigenvalues govern everything), and the unconditional mean is $(I - \Phi)^{-1}\mu$. Every "mean reversion" statement in this course is one of these two formulas read aloud.

In the paper's empirical implementation the vector is six-dimensional, $X_t = (g_t,\ \beta_t,\ \Delta po_t,\ r_t,\ cay_t,\ \pi_t)'$ — dividend growth, the time-varying beta, the change in the payout ratio (its earnings-growth proxy), the nominal short rate, Lettau–Ludvigson $cay$, and inflation. The full model is the six-variable version of (3b):

$$X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t + \Sigma \varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t+1} \sim N(0, I)$$
(3)

Term · affine

"Affine" sounds formidable; it means "a constant plus a linear function." That is all. A function is affine in $X$ if it equals $a + b'X$ — some fixed number $a$, plus each element of $X$ multiplied by its own coefficient. Growth being "affine in the state" simply says: expected growth = a baseline $\delta_0^g$, plus adjustments $\delta_1^g$ for wherever the economy currently is. No powers, no products of variables, no curves — a straight (hyper)plane. The word matters because it names the whole model class: if prices are exponentials of affine things, everything stays computable in closed form. Whenever you read "affine" in this course, mentally substitute "constant-plus-linear" and nothing is lost.

The model assumes both quantities are affine in the state:

$$g_{t+1} = \delta_0^g + \delta_1^{g\,\prime} X_{t+1}$$
(4)
$$r_{t+1} = \delta_0^r + \delta_1^{r\,\prime} X_{t}$$
(5)

Note the timing

$r_{t+1}$ is known at $t$ — it is the expected return, part of the information set. $g_{t+1}$ is realized at $t+1$. This asymmetry is what makes the model a model of expected-return variation rather than realized-return noise.

Why a VAR is the right solution here

Four requirements, four matches:

1 · Jointness by construction

One $\Sigma\Sigma'$ generates the shocks to cash flows and expected returns together. The covariance the price needs (Step 04) falls out of the estimate — it cannot be set to zero by accident, because there is nowhere to set it.

2 · Mean reversion for free

A stable $\Phi$ (eigenvalues inside the unit circle) delivers $E_t[r_{t+j}]$ gliding back to $\bar r$ at a speed the data choose. The flat-forever discount rate is the special case $\Phi = 0$, $X$ frozen — Damodaran lives in that corner.

3 · Predictability is symmetric and testable

Do short rates forecast dividend growth? Does $cay$ forecast betas? These are coefficients of $\Phi$, with standard errors. The dynamic relations are not assumed — they are estimated and can be rejected.

4 · Gaussian linearity makes the price closed-form

Linear dynamics + normal shocks ⇒ every $\sum(g - r)$ over every horizon is conditionally normal ⇒ $E[\exp(\cdot)]$ is the two-line lognormal formula of Step 04. Change the distribution and you lose the analytic price — this is the load-bearing constraint.

A natural worry: VAR(1) means one lag — is that restrictive? No: any VAR(p) can be rewritten as a bigger VAR(1) using the companion form (stack the last $p$ values of $X$ into a taller vector). The paper says exactly this. The honest constraint is not the lag count but the linearity + Gaussianity, which buys the closed form at the price of no stochastic volatility in the state dynamics.

Term · mean reversion (and eigenvalues)

A variable mean-reverts if, when it sits above its long-run average, it tends to fall back toward it (and vice versa) — like a rubber band. In the VAR, the speed of the snap-back is governed by the eigenvalues of $\Phi$. An eigenvalue is just a number summarizing how much a matrix shrinks (or stretches) certain directions of the state. If all eigenvalues are between −1 and 1, the system is stable and today's conditions fade geometrically; an eigenvalue of 0.9 means a deviation is still 90% alive after one period, and $0.9^{10} \approx 35\%$ alive after ten. Eigenvalues near 1 = long memory ("persistent"); near 0 = fast forgetting. That single number is why "persistence" pervades this course.

Step 04 · The lognormal step

Where the magic happens

Since $X$ is Gaussian and $g, r$ are affine in $X$, the sum $S_j \equiv \sum_{i=1}^j (g_{t+i} - r_{t+i})$ is conditionally normal.

Term · conditionally normal / lognormal

Conditionally normal means: given what we know today ($X_t$), the uncertain quantity is normally distributed — its mean and variance depend on today's state, but its shape is the familiar bell curve. Why does that help? Because sums of normal things are normal, and the whole horizon-$j$ object $S_j$ inherits it. The step from there to a price uses the one fact worth memorizing from this course: if $S$ is normal, then $e^S$ is lognormal — always positive, right-skewed — and its average is not $e^{E[S]}$ but $E[e^S] = e^{E[S] + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}(S)}$. The average of the exponential beats the exponential of the average, and the gap grows with variance. That gap is the "new part" in equation (6): uncertainty itself moves the price.

Term · Jensen's inequality (convexity)

Why does uncertainty raise value here? Because the pricing function $S \mapsto e^S$ is convex — it curves upward. Jensen's inequality is the formal statement: the average of a curved function exceeds the function at the average ($E[f(S)] \geq f(E[S])$ when $f$ curves up). Concrete version: your stock is worth 110 if things go well, 90 if badly (average 100); the payoff $e^S$ on those states averages more than the payoff at the average state. Upside gains outweigh symmetric downside losses. This is the same mathematics that makes bond convexity and option time value positive — here it makes cash-flow uncertainty a plus in value, before risk pricing kicks in.

Applying that fact to $S_j$ gives the strip value:

$$E_t\left[e^{S_j}\right] = \exp\Big( \underbrace{E_t[S_j]}_{\text{Damodaran's part}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}_t[S_j]}_{\text{the new part}} \Big)$$
(6)

Now expand the variance term:

$$\mathrm{Var}_t[S_j] = \mathrm{Var}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{j} g_{t+i}\Big] + \mathrm{Var}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{t+i}\Big] - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{j} g_{t+i},\ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{t+i}\Big]$$
(7)

(Shorthand below: $\sum g$ and $\sum r$ for those two cumulated sums — sums over horizons, not to be confused with the shock matrix $\Sigma$ of equation (3).)

Read equation (7) carefully — this is the punchline

  • Damodaran computes only $E_t[S_j]$: point-forecast growth minus a constant rate.
  • Uncertainty about growth ($\mathrm{Var}[\sum g]$) raises value — convexity, because value is exponential in growth.
  • Uncertainty about discount rates also raises value, for the same convexity reason.
  • The covariance term is the economically important one. If good news about growth arrives together with higher discount rates — which is what the aggregate data show, cf. the positive correlation between expected returns and expected dividend growth in Van Binsbergen–Koijen — then $\mathrm{Cov} > 0$, the $-2\,\mathrm{Cov}$ term is negative, and value is lower than any DCF that ignores the interaction.

This is the precise, quantitative answer to the question why model cash flows and discount rates jointly. It isn't only about variance decomposition. The joint distribution enters the price level.

Step 05 · The solution

The closed-form solution

Substituting (3)–(5) into (2) and collecting terms, each horizon's contribution is exponential-affine in the state:

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \exp\left( a_j + b_j^{\prime} X_t \right)$$
(8)

where $(a_j, b_j)$ obey recursions of the form

$$b_{j+1}^{\prime} = b_j^{\prime}\Phi + \left(\delta_1^{g\,\prime}\Phi - \delta_1^{r\,\prime}\right)$$
(9)
$$a_{j+1} = a_j + b_j^{\prime}\mu + \left(\delta_0^g - \delta_0^r\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{b}_j^{\prime} \Sigma\Sigma^{\prime} \tilde{b}_j$$
(10)

(the exact constants depend on the timing convention; the structure is what matters). This is the same recursive architecture as affine term-structure models — Vasicek, Duffie–Kan — applied to equity.

Honesty about the special case

The paper's most general expected return is quadratic in the state. (From here on the paper's own symbol $\mu_t$ denotes the one-period expected return — our $r_{t+1}$; the $\mu$ of equation (3) retires from duty.) The general form is $\mu_t = \alpha + \xi' X_t + X_t' \Omega X_t$, because a conditional CAPM multiplies two stochastic objects: $\mu_t = \alpha + r_t + \beta_t \lambda_t$. When both $\beta_t$ and $\lambda_t$ move, the product $\beta_t(b_1' z_t)$ puts a $K \times K$ matrix $\Omega \neq 0$ into the expected return, and the price picks up an extra recursion $H(n)$, so each strip becomes $\exp\left(a_n + b_n' X_t + X_t' H_n' X_t\right)$ — exponential-quadratic, the equity cousin of the quadratic-Gaussian term-structure models of Constantinides (1992) and Ahn–Dittmar–Gallant (2002). The affine solution above is exact whenever $\Omega = 0$: a constant beta with a time-varying premium, or a time-varying beta with a constant premium — the cases the earlier affine literature (Ang–Liu 2001, Bakshi–Chen 2001, Bekaert–Grenadier 2001) could handle. What none of those models could do is let both move at once. That simultaneity is the 2004 paper's core contribution.

No log-linearization anywhere

The $\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{b}^{\prime} \Sigma\Sigma^{\prime} \tilde{b}$ term in (10) is the Jensen/covariance adjustment accumulating at every horizon. The solution is exact within the lognormal-affine class — which is why it doesn't break for high-growth or extreme-multiple firms the way Campbell–Shiller does.

What is a log-linearization? Campbell–Shiller's classic framework needs the (curved, non-linear) relation between prices, dividends and returns to become a straight line, so they approximate it by a first-order Taylor expansion around a point — accurate near that point, drifting when prices or growth are far from "typical" (exactly the growth-stock case). Ang–Liu work in logs from the start, where the same relations are exactly linear, so no approximation is ever taken: the closed form below is exact, not a local approximation.

Step 06 · The terminal value

What replaces the terminal value

Gordon growth is the degenerate case: set $\Phi = 0$, $\Sigma = 0$, then $b_j = 0$, $a_j = j(g - r)$, and

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_j e^{\,j(g-r)} = \frac{e^{\,g-r}}{1 - e^{\,g-r}} \;\approx\; \frac{1+g}{r-g}$$
(11)

So Gordon is Ang–Liu with zero persistence, zero volatility, zero correlation. Convergence in the general case requires the eigenvalues of $\Phi$ inside the unit circle and $a_j + b_j^{\prime}X_t$ eventually declining — the analogue of $r > g$, but now a condition on the dynamics rather than on two point estimates.

Step 07 · The bridge to practice

Spot discount rates: keeping the two-step workflow

The closed-form price is elegant, but practice does valuation in two steps: forecast $E_t[D_{t+n}]$ first (from projections or analyst estimates), then discount. The closed form of Step 05 (the paper's Proposition I.1) mixes the two inseparably — you cannot pull a "cash flow forecast" out of the left-hand side of (1) without also mixing in the whole distribution of discount rates. The paper's solution is to keep the practitioner's workflow and change only the second step. Instead of one rate, define a whole term structure of discount rates $\{\mu_t(n)\}$, one per maturity, by:

$$E_t\left[\left(\prod_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{-\mu_{t+k}}\right) D_{t+n}\right] \;=\; \frac{E_t[D_{t+n}]}{\exp\left(n \cdot \mu_t(n)\right)}$$
(12)

The left side is exactly one term of the pricing sum (1). The right side says: discount the expected cash flow at horizon $n$ at the single horizon-specific rate $\mu_t(n)$ — the "spot expected return." Summing over $n$ recovers the full price. Because the definition equates a distributional object to a point-discounting, each $\mu_t(n)$ internally contains the covariance corrections of Step 4 — the practitioner never sees them, but they are priced in.

In the quadratic model, $\mu_t(n) = A(n) + B(n)' X_t + X_t' G(n) X_t$: the discount curve itself is a quadratic function of today's state, computable in closed form, and $\mu_t(n) \to \bar\mu$ as $n \to \infty$ under stationarity. Like the bond yield curve, it can slope up, down, or be humped — and it moves with the economy. Unlike the bond curve, it is not directly observable: the paper notes one could in principle back it out of dividend-strip derivatives, which barely existed in 2004 (more on that in Step 10's link to Van Binsbergen–Koijen).

Term · term structure

A term structure is just "the whole set of rates, one per horizon, drawn as a curve." You already know it from bonds: the yield curve plots the interest rate for 1-year, 2-year, … 30-year lending. The phrase "term structure of discount rates" imports exactly that picture to equity: instead of one WACC for all horizons, there is a rate for cash arriving in year 1, another for year 2, and so on — and the shape of that curve (upward, downward, humped) is economically informative, exactly as a steep vs inverted yield curve is. "n" throughout this section is the horizon ("maturity"/"term") each rate applies to.

Why this is the paper's most practical idea

It answers the objection "no manager will estimate a joint VAR of cash flows and expected returns." Nobody has to. Estimate the VAR once (the paper does it on portfolio data); read off the discount curve $\{\mu_t(n)\}$; then discount any separately-forecast cash-flow path, horizon by horizon. The Damodaran workflow survives intact — only the single WACC is replaced by a curve. Brennan (1997) had proposed a term structure of discount rates but required simulation and constant betas; Ang–Liu make it analytic and let beta move.

Step 08 · Estimation

From theory to numbers: how everything is estimated

The model is only useful if every object in it can be measured. It can. The paper's pipeline runs from raw data to a priced discount curve in five stages — and the remarkable fact is that nothing in it is exotic: every stage is a first-year-graduate econometrics exercise.

Term · Newey–West (and why overlapping data needs it)

The paper measures annual growth using monthly data. Consequence: each observation shares 11 of its 12 months with the next one — adjacent errors are mechanically correlated ("overlapping"). Ordinary standard errors assume independent errors and would overstate confidence. Newey–West standard errors are the standard correction: they estimate the covariance of the errors over a window of lags (12 here, one per overlapping month) and inflate the uncertainty accordingly. Read "Newey–West (12 lags)" as: "standard errors honest about the overlap."

  1. 1

    Build the observables

    Cash flow growth $g_t$: for each portfolio, monthly dividends are backed out of the difference between returns with and without dividends, then summed over 12 months to remove seasonality; $g_t = \log(D_t/D_{t-1})$. Short rate $r_t$: the one-year zero-coupon rate. $cay_t$: Lettau–Ludvigson's consumption–wealth–labor deviation, re-estimated recursively using only data up to each $t$ — no look-ahead. $\pi_t$: year-on-year CPI inflation. Payout $\Delta po_t$: from COMPUSTAT annual dividends and earnings (firms with negative earnings excluded).

  2. 2

    Estimate the time-varying betas

    Rolling 60-month regressions of each portfolio's log excess return on the market's log excess return. The sloping coefficient in each window is $\beta_t$ — a data series, not an assumption. (The paper notes this is the standard Fama–MacBeth-era method, deliberately simple: Kalman filters or Gibbs samplers would be more efficient but obscure the point.)

  3. 3

    Estimate the risk-premium regression

    One predictive regression of annual market excess returns on the short rate and $cay$: $y^m_{t+1} - r_t = b_0 + b_r r_t + b_{cay}\,cay_t + \epsilon_{t+1}$. Its fitted value defines $\lambda_t$. In the sample the premium has unconditional volatility 2.66% and autocorrelation 0.54 — measurable, moving, persistent.

  4. 4

    Estimate the VAR

    Stack $X_t = (g_t,\ \beta_t,\ \Delta po_t,\ r_t,\ cay_t,\ \pi_t)'$ and regress each variable on all six lagged variables — equation (3) is six linear regressions. Estimated on monthly, overlapping annual-horizon data with Newey–West (12-lag) standard errors, portfolio by portfolio. The output is $\hat\mu$, $\hat\Phi$, $\hat\Sigma$: the entire law of motion, with confidence intervals. The $\alpha$ in $\mu_t = \alpha + r_t + \beta_t\lambda_t$ is set so the model matches each portfolio's average excess return.

  5. 5

    Compute and read off the outputs

    Plug $(\hat\mu, \hat\Phi, \hat\Sigma)$ and December 2000's $X_t$ into the recursions of Step 05 → the price (8) and the discount curve $\mu_t(n)$ of Step 07. Decompose $\mathrm{var}(\mu_t(n))$ through the state variables → what moves prices, by horizon (Step 10).

The division of labor

Cash flows and expected returns are estimated by different standard tools (dividend accounting; rolling regressions and a predictive regression), but they are then glued together inside one VAR. That is the answer to "how do you concretely estimate it": each piece is ordinary; the innovation is refusing to keep the pieces in separate drawers. The VAR is where the covariance that pricing requires gets estimated rather than assumed away.

What could go wrong — and does

Every input is a statistic, not a fact. Rolling betas are noisy (annual volatility 10–19% across portfolios — betas genuinely move). The premium regression's coefficients are imprecise at annual horizons; $cay$'s predictive power weakens without look-ahead. And the VAR's persistence parameters inherit the Stambaugh small-sample bias that the Caveat step takes up. The framework tells you where the fragile links are — which is itself an advantage over a WACC asserted from a table of industry betas.

Step 09 · The field

The mental map: where the state variables come from

Step 08 showed you how the VAR is estimated; this step places that exercise on the map of the field. The VAR itself is only a frame — a grammar for joint dynamics. Everything interesting about which variables sit in $X_t = (g_t,\ \beta_t,\ \Delta po_t,\ r_t,\ cay_t,\ \pi_t)'$ is a judgment call inherited from two neighboring literatures: the literature that links firm profitability to state variables (the cash-flow side) and the literature that links expected returns to state variables (the discount-rate side). Ang–Liu's contribution is the joint frame; the frame's content comes from these two maps, and any future improvement of the model will come from improving them.

Territory 1 — what moves firm profitability (the cash-flow side)

The cash-flow side of the VAR is not an accounting identity — it is the profitability-forecasting literature, whose central findings are decades old and remarkably stable:

Profitability mean-reverts — and is forecastable

Fama & French (2000) show that profitability (earnings on book equity) is highly forecastable and mean-reverts toward economy-wide levels over 5–10 years — expected profitability is predicted by fundamentals, and value firms have persistently higher expected profitability than growth firms. This is exactly the AR structure the VAR imposes on $g_t$: persistence $\Phi$, pull $\mu$.

ROE has internal structure, linked to rates

Nissim & Penman (2001) decompose ROE into margin and turnover, each with its own dynamics and mean reversion — and document that interest rates predict subsequent profitability with a negative sign (rising rates, squeezed margins). The negative $\alpha_1$ of the 2001 companion paper is this finding, built in.

At the firm level, cash-flow news dominates

Vuolteenaho (2002) decomposes firm-level stock returns and finds cash-flow news, not expected-return news, drives the large majority of firm-level return variance. The joint distribution the VAR prices is first-order for firms — not a refinement.

Profitability persistence prices into multiples

Ang & Liu (2001) show price-to-book rises with the persistence of profitability and growth — more mean-reverting firms deserve lower multiples. Lee, Myers & Swaminathan (1999) show residual-income values track prices, and that expected-return variation drives much of book-to-market's movement.

The map teaches a lesson about method: the paper's state choice is itself an empirical judgment. Dividend yield — the most famous predictor — was dropped because the literature had shown its predictive power collapsed in the 1990s; the short rate and $cay$ were kept because that same literature showed they survive. Selecting $X_t$ is applied predictability research, not free taste.

The bridge territory — why joint dynamics have economic content

Two structural literatures explain why the two territories share a border at all — why the same state variables plausibly drive both cash flows and discount rates:

Long-run risk

Bansal & Yaron (2004): when growth contains a persistent component and volatility moves, discount rates and cash-flow expectations interact at long horizons — the structural reason the persistence of $g_t$ (not just its mean) belongs in a valuation model.

Productivity as the common source

Croce (2014): long-run productivity risk simultaneously generates cash-flow dynamics and discount-rate variation in general equilibrium — the deep economic story for why one state vector can legitimately drive both sides of the VAR.

Above both territories stands Cochrane's (2011) field-level statement — "discount rate variation is the central organizing question of current asset pricing" — and below them lies the market test: dividend strips (Van Binsbergen & Koijen 2017) measure the very term structure of discount rates this framework computes.

How to read the whole field in one sentence

The profitability literature tells you what forecasts $g_t$ (mean-reverting ROE, payout, rates with a negative sign); the predictability literature tells you what forecasts $\mu_t$ (rates, $cay$, moving betas — not dividend yields); the structural literature tells you why the same macro state can drive both; and the VAR is the single frame where those two empirical territories are forced to be consistent with each other — because price requires their joint distribution.

The map is also the research frontier

Nothing in the frame is closed. A new candidate state variable enters exactly the way the old ones did: show it predicts cash flows or expected returns in the regressions of Step 08, establish its dynamics, and slot it into $X_t$. That is precisely how the modern extensions proceed — text-based and news-based indicators of policy and climate risk (in the spirit of Engle, Giglio, Kelly, Lee & Stroebel, 2020), firm-level big-data profitability signals, or heterogeneous climate exposures all enter as new columns of the same VAR. The framework does not compete with those literatures; it is the place where their indicators become prices.

Step 10 · The evidence

What the paper actually finds

The empirical implementation estimates the VAR at an annual horizon on monthly, overlapping data (Newey–West corrected) for ten book-to-market portfolios and 46 industry portfolios (the Fama–French 49 minus Health, Miscellaneous and Utilities), with betas from 60-month rolling regressions (so the VAR sample runs from 1970, after the beta burn-in, through December 2000). Three results matter for a valuator.

1The discount curve is steeply upward-sloping when the risk premium is low

At the end of December 2000 — the sample's end, with the market at its peak — the short rate and $cay$ both predicted unusually low market returns. The short end of the discount curve collapsed accordingly; the long end mean-reverted back up. The result is a steeply upward-sloping term structure of discount rates, lying far below the constant rate an unconditional CAPM would have picked. A DCF that used the historical-average WACC at that date was discounting every horizon at a rate far above the model's short-rate-implied one.

2Mis-pricings of a $1 perpetuity are large — up to −58%

Valuing a perpetuity of $1 per year under each portfolio's discount curve, the paper compares the full model against three wrong models. The traditional constant-rate DDM produces a mean error of −15.3% across the ten book-to-market portfolios (worst decile: −32.0%) and −16.9% across industries (Ships: −57.9%, Fabricated products: −32.9%). Decomposing the error: ignoring time-variation in the risk premium costs −13.4% on average (B/M) — roughly three times the cost of ignoring time-varying beta (−1.5%). In 2000, getting the premium right mattered far more than getting beta right.

3What drives discount-rate variation depends on the horizon

A variance decomposition of the spot discount rate $\mu_t(n)$ (Corollary II.2) shows the anatomy. At short horizons the risk premium dominates: at $n = 1$, roughly 65% of $\mathrm{var}(\mu_t)$ comes from risk-free rates, 72% from the market risk premium, and only 20% from beta (neutral portfolio; the decomposition double-counts covariances, so the shares need not sum to 100). But the premium is the least persistent piece (autocorrelation ≈ 0.54, vs ≈ 0.74 for the short rate), so its share decays with horizon while the persistent pieces — the short rate, and beta for value stocks — take over at long maturities. Cash-flow variables contribute almost nothing to discount-rate variation at any horizon.

The intuition is one line of algebra. Write $\mu_t = r_t + \beta_t\lambda_t$, decompose around means, and drop the interaction: $\mathrm{var}(\mu_t) \approx \mathrm{var}(r_t) + \bar\beta^2\,\mathrm{var}(\lambda_t) + \bar\lambda^2\,\mathrm{var}(\beta_t)$. The short rate enters one-for-one; the premium is scaled by $\bar\beta \approx 1$; beta's variance is scaled by the average log premium $\bar\lambda \approx 5\%$, which crushes its weight — unless, as for value stocks, $\mathrm{var}(\beta_t)$ is big enough to break through.

Why this approach turns price-watching into price-explaining

The alternative to this framework is not a different model — it is guessing with structure: pick a WACC from a table, pick a terminal growth rate, run three scenarios, adjust by feel. Every claim about what drives the valuation is then untestable, because nothing in the procedure pins down where a number came from. The joint VAR approach replaces each guess with a measured object and each narrative with an accounting identity. "Rates are low today so growth stocks should be worth more" becomes a computed term structure $E_t[r_{t+j}]$ whose slope depends on estimated persistence. "Uncertainty is high so I add a premium" becomes the sign-disciplined $\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}$ and $-2\,\mathrm{Cov}$ terms. "This stock is riskier" becomes a beta series with measured volatility and persistence. And when the valuation is wrong, you can trace which link failed — premium regression, beta dynamics, or the cash-flow process — instead of re-arguing the whole model over coffee. That is what "understanding empirically what drives prices" means operationally: not better intuition about prices, but a decomposition of price into named, estimated, criticizable parts.

Step 11 · The companion paper

Ang–Liu (2001): the same idea in accounting clothes

The repo's second paper — A General Affine Earnings Valuation Model, Review of Accounting Studies — runs the same logic through the residual income model (RIM) instead of the dividend model, and it is worth seeing both because they fail in beautifully symmetric ways. Value is book value plus discounted abnormal earnings,

$$S_t = y_t + \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} E_t^Q\left[\left(\prod_{j=0}^{i-1} e^{-r_{t+j}}\right) xa_{t+i}\right], \qquad xa_t = x_t - \left(e^{r_{t-1}} - 1\right)y_{t-1}$$
(13)

where $y_t$ is book value, $x_t$ earnings, and the expectation is taken under the risk-neutral measure generated by a log-normal pricing kernel with prices of risk $\gamma$ — both defined just below. The constant-rate RIM is the special case where the kernel is deterministic, the accounting mirror of Step 01's punchline. And the paper sharpens that mirror:

Corollary 2.2, Ang–Liu (2001)

It is not always possible, even with constant interest rates, to capture risk aversion by adding a constant spread to the discount rate. A constant discount factor works only if there is no homoskedastic risk premium ($h\gamma$) and no non-zero-mean state dynamics. The "risk-adjusted WACC" is not a simplification — under moving second moments, it is an error.

Two phrases in that corollary — pricing kernel and homoskedastic risk premium — carry the whole argument, so let us open them:

Term · pricing kernel and risk-neutral measure

A pricing kernel (or stochastic discount factor) is a single random variable $m$ that prices everything: every price is an expected product, $P = E[m \times \text{payoff}]$. It exists under no-arbitrage alone — no theory of risk needed yet. Where does risk enter? Bad states (recessions) get high $m$: payoffs landing there are worth relatively more, so assets that pay poorly in bad states must be cheap — that is risk pricing. The risk-neutral measure is a bookkeeping trick built on it: instead of weighting payoffs by $m$, distort the probabilities so that pricing becomes "expected payoff under new probabilities, discounted at the risk-free rate." Same arithmetic, friendlier format — $E^Q$ in the formula above is exactly that distortion. "Prices of risk" $\gamma$ measure how hard the probabilities are distorted per unit of each shock.

Term · heteroskedasticity

A series is homoskedastic if its volatility is constant over time, and heteroskedastic if volatility itself moves — calm quarters, wild quarters. (Homo = same; hetero = different; skedaston = scatter/variance.) Equity returns, interest rates, and firms' earnings are visibly heteroskedastic: volatility clusters. This matters twice in the 2001 paper: moving volatility is a reason the risk premium must be state-dependent (a constant spread cannot represent it — Corollary 2.2), and it is why the affine dynamics there let the conditional covariance itself move with the state ($\sigma_t\sigma_t' = h + H \cdot X_t$).

Two results translate directly to valuation practice:

Affine goodwill survives stochastic rates

Goodwill $g_t = S_t - y_t = \alpha_t + \beta_t' X_t$ remains affine in accounting variables — but the coefficients are time-varying, loading on the current zero-coupon curve, and under heteroskedasticity the risk premium enters the linear terms (state-dependent premia). Linearity survives only if rates are independent of accounting variables.

Price-to-book: persistence raises multiples

With $X_t = (r_t, z_t, g_t)'$ — short rate, abnormal return, growth in book — the P/B ratio is a closed-form non-linear function. Comparative statics: higher profitability and growth raise P/B, and more persistent profitability or growth raises P/B — highly mean-reverting firms deserve lower multiples, a quantitative anchor for the "duration" intuition of Step 10.

The Jensen vs. risk-aversion horse race

The 2001 paper's most striking comparative static: under risk neutrality, raising any state volatility — even the interest rate's — increases price-to-book, purely through Jensen's inequality (convexity, the $\frac12 \mathrm{Var}$ term of Step 04). Volatility only lowers value once it is priced ($\gamma < 0$): in the Intel calibration, growth-in-book volatility lowers P/B because the price of growth risk is large ($\gamma_3 = -13.2$), while interest-rate volatility still raises it because interest-rate risk is essentially unpriced. The Damodaran habit of "adding a risk premium for uncertainty" has a sign problem: for unpriced risk, uncertainty adds value.

Consequences

The three things that concretely change

1The discount curve tilts

If expected returns are currently above their long-run mean and mean-revert with persistence $\phi \approx 0.9$, then $E_t[r_{t+10}]$ is close to the unconditional mean while $E_t[r_{t+1}]$ is high. Damodaran uses today's high rate forever and systematically undervalues. The reverse in low-rate regimes: he uses today's low rate forever and overvalues. The error scales with the asset's duration — worst exactly for the growth names where DCF is already most fragile.

2Cash flow forecasts get a distribution, not a path

Damodaran's "conservative case" scenario table is an informal substitute for the $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}$ term. Ang–Liu makes it analytic — and shows the direction of the correction depends on the sign of the covariance, which no scenario table gives you.

3Duration and risk become linked

Because $b_j$ varies with horizon, the sensitivity of value to the state differs across the cash-flow strip — which is precisely the object Van Binsbergen–Koijen went and measured with dividend derivatives. Ang–Liu is the theoretical frame; dividend strips are the empirical test of whether the implied term structure is right.

Limitations

The honest caveat

Everything hinges on $\hat{\Phi}$ and $\hat{\Sigma}$. The recursion in (9) accumulates $\Phi^j$, so an overestimate of persistence — and Stambaugh bias guarantees you overestimate it in short samples with persistent predictors — compounds into large valuation errors at long horizons.

You've traded Damodaran's transparent assumptions (a WACC you can argue about over coffee) for statistical assumptions that are harder to interrogate and just as consequential. Use the model to understand the shape of the discount curve and the sign of the growth–rate interaction; be skeptical of the third decimal place.

The authors themselves flag the road ahead: parameter uncertainty in the risk-premium predictability and in estimated betas affects the capital-budgeting problem, and time-varying rates, betas and premiums "can only make potential mis-pricings in these situations even larger." Estimation risk does not shrink the errors — it compounds them.

A final caution the papers make explicit: the beta you put into the VAR and the beta the model's implied returns give back out are not automatically the same object (the implied-return beta carries a small Jensen correction), and the model implies a valuation that need not equal the observed market price — the framework prices consistently, it does not guarantee the market agrees.
