Step 00 · The benchmark

The DCF you already know

Damodaran's DCF is the workhorse of every valuation course and every banker's model:

$$V_t = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{E_t[D_{t+j}]}{(1+r)^j}$$
(0)

with a constant WACC $r$, and then a Gordon terminal value $D_{t+1}/(r-g)$ bolted on at the end. Two assumptions are buried inside that innocuous-looking line:

1

One $r$ for all horizons

A flat, constant discount curve. The rate you use to discount year 1 is the rate you use to discount year 40.

2

Expectation of the ratio ≈ ratio of expectations

Cash flows and discount rates are treated as independent. The discount factor simply slides out of the expectation.

Both are wrong, and Ang–Liu shows how wrong.

Step 01 · The definition

The correct definition of value

Value is the expectation of the discounted cash flow — with the discount factor inside the expectation:

$$V_t = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} E_t\left[ D_{t+j} \cdot \exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{t+i}\right) \right]$$
(1)

where $r_{t+i}$ is the (log) one-period expected return from $t+i-1$ to $t+i$ — itself random as of time $t$.

The whole paper in one line

Damodaran's formula pulls the discount factor out of the expectation. That is only legitimate if $r$ is deterministic. Once expected returns move, $E[XY] \neq E[X]\,E[Y]$.

Everything that follows is just bookkeeping to make equation (1) computable — without ever pulling the discount factor back out.

Step 02 · Growth form

Write cash flows in growth form

Let $g_{t+i} = \log(D_{t+i}/D_{t+i-1})$. Then $D_{t+j} = D_t \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^j g_{t+i}\right)$, and (1) becomes

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} E_t\left[ \exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j} \left(g_{t+i} - r_{t+i}\right)\right) \right]$$
(2)

Value is now a price–dividend ratio: a sum over horizons of the expected exponential of cumulated growth-minus-discount-rate. One strip per horizon — exactly the objects that trade, in fragmented form, in the dividend-derivatives market.

Step 03 · Dynamics

The VAR: where dynamics enter

Collect the state variables in $X_t$ (an $N\times 1$ vector — e.g. dividend growth, ROE, book-to-market, a short rate, a spread). Assume a Gaussian VAR(1):

$$X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t + \Sigma \varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t+1} \sim N(0, I)$$
(3)

Then assume both quantities are affine in the state:

$$g_{t+1} = \delta_0^g + \delta_1^{g\,\prime} X_{t+1}$$
(4)
$$r_{t+1} = \delta_0^r + \delta_1^{r\,\prime} X_{t}$$
(5)

Note the timing

$r_{t+1}$ is known at $t$ — it is the expected return, part of the information set. $g_{t+1}$ is realized at $t+1$. This asymmetry is what makes the model a model of expected-return variation rather than realized-return noise.

Three things this buys you:

Mean reversion

$E_t[X_{t+j}] = (I + \Phi + \dots + \Phi^{j-1})\mu + \Phi^j X_t$. Expected returns drift back to their unconditional mean at a rate governed by $\Phi$'s eigenvalues. Damodaran's $r$ never moves.

Correlation

$\Sigma\Sigma'$ off-diagonals let shocks to growth and shocks to discount rates be correlated. Damodaran has no such object.

Term structure

$\Phi^j$ makes the $j$-horizon forecast differ from the 1-horizon one, for both $g$ and $r$.

Step 04 · The lognormal step

Where the magic happens

Since $X$ is Gaussian and $g, r$ are affine in $X$, the sum $S_j \equiv \sum_{i=1}^j (g_{t+i} - r_{t+i})$ is conditionally normal. For a normal variable, $E[e^S] = \exp\left(E[S] + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}[S]\right)$. So:

$$E_t\left[e^{S_j}\right] = \exp\Big( \underbrace{E_t[S_j]}_{\text{Damodaran's part}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}_t[S_j]}_{\text{the new part}} \Big)$$
(6)

Now expand the variance term:

$$\mathrm{Var}_t[S_j] = \mathrm{Var}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum g\Big] + \mathrm{Var}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum r\Big] - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}_t\Big[\textstyle\sum g, \textstyle\sum r\Big]$$
(7)

Read equation (7) carefully — this is the punchline

  • Damodaran computes only $E_t[S_j]$: point-forecast growth minus a constant rate.
  • Uncertainty about growth ($\mathrm{Var}[\sum g]$) raises value — convexity, because value is exponential in growth.
  • Uncertainty about discount rates also raises value, for the same convexity reason.
  • The covariance term is the economically important one. If good news about growth arrives together with higher discount rates — which is what the aggregate data show, cf. the positive correlation between expected returns and expected dividend growth in Van Binsbergen–Koijen — then $\mathrm{Cov} > 0$, the $-2\,\mathrm{Cov}$ term is negative, and value is lower than any DCF that ignores the interaction.

This is the precise, quantitative answer to the question why model cash flows and discount rates jointly. It isn't only about variance decomposition. The joint distribution enters the price level.

Step 05 · The solution

The closed-form solution

Substituting (3)–(5) into (2) and collecting terms, each horizon's contribution is exponential-affine in the state:

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \exp\left( a_j + b_j^{\prime} X_t \right)$$
(8)

where $(a_j, b_j)$ obey recursions of the form

$$b_{j+1}^{\prime} = b_j^{\prime}\Phi + \left(\delta_1^{g\,\prime}\Phi - \delta_1^{r\,\prime}\right)$$
(9)
$$a_{j+1} = a_j + b_j^{\prime}\mu + \left(\delta_0^g - \delta_0^r\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{b}_j^{\prime} \Sigma\Sigma^{\prime} \tilde{b}_j$$
(10)

(the exact constants depend on the timing convention; the structure is what matters). This is the same recursive architecture as affine term-structure models — Vasicek, Duffie–Kan — applied to equity.

No log-linearization anywhere

The $\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{b}^{\prime} \Sigma\Sigma^{\prime} \tilde{b}$ term in (10) is the Jensen/covariance adjustment accumulating at every horizon. The solution is exact within the lognormal-affine class — which is why it doesn't break for high-growth or extreme-multiple firms the way Campbell–Shiller does.

Step 06 · The terminal value

What replaces the terminal value

Gordon growth is the degenerate case: set $\Phi = 0$, $\Sigma = 0$, then $b_j = 0$, $a_j = j(g - r)$, and

$$\frac{V_t}{D_t} = \sum_j e^{\,j(g-r)} = \frac{e^{\,g-r}}{1 - e^{\,g-r}} \;\approx\; \frac{1+g}{r-g}$$
(11)

So Gordon is Ang–Liu with zero persistence, zero volatility, zero correlation. Convergence in the general case requires the eigenvalues of $\Phi$ inside the unit circle and $a_j + b_j^{\prime}X_t$ eventually declining — the analogue of $r > g$, but now a condition on the dynamics rather than on two point estimates.

Consequences

The three things that concretely change

1The discount curve tilts

If expected returns are currently above their long-run mean and mean-revert with persistence $\phi \approx 0.9$, then $E_t[r_{t+10}]$ is close to the unconditional mean while $E_t[r_{t+1}]$ is high. Damodaran uses today's high rate forever and systematically undervalues. The reverse in low-rate regimes: he uses today's low rate forever and overvalues. The error scales with the asset's duration — worst exactly for the growth names where DCF is already most fragile.

2Cash flow forecasts get a distribution, not a path

Damodaran's "conservative case" scenario table is an informal substitute for the $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}$ term. Ang–Liu makes it analytic — and shows the direction of the correction depends on the sign of the covariance, which no scenario table gives you.

3Duration and risk become linked

Because $b_j$ varies with horizon, the sensitivity of value to the state differs across the cash-flow strip — which is precisely the object Van Binsbergen–Koijen went and measured with dividend derivatives. Ang–Liu is the theoretical frame; dividend strips are the empirical test of whether the implied term structure is right.

Limitations

The honest caveat

Everything hinges on $\hat{\Phi}$ and $\hat{\Sigma}$. The recursion in (9) accumulates $\Phi^j$, so an overestimate of persistence — and Stambaugh bias guarantees you overestimate it in short samples with persistent predictors — compounds into large valuation errors at long horizons.

You've traded Damodaran's transparent assumptions (a WACC you can argue about over coffee) for statistical assumptions that are harder to interrogate and just as consequential. Use the model to understand the shape of the discount curve and the sign of the growth–rate interaction; be skeptical of the third decimal place.
